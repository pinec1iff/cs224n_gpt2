Cupid laid by his brand and fell asleep.
A maid of Dian's this advantage found,
And his love-kindling fire did quickly steep;
O, what beauty could be hid if found;
The eyes were fair of shape, brows so white,
Serene in white their curls fell, their fair faces in
Darkness black like a night:
So beauty alone hid he that lay by!
The eyes being so black his beauty in them made
The eyes white before them in form that was his love,
Therefore that he which saw him doth dwell.
  Then love being so, having another's night he doth,
  Being night and living by day his eyes seem his days and parts of days.

When my love swears that she is made of truth,
I do believe her, though I know she lies,
That she might think me some untutored youth,
Making these new praises of myself.
Then do I think nothing more of the book that
I read, which doth give more joy than life can give
Without rhyme or reason.
The best is the one that I make, or else with thought
That doth delight me; for each is in another place.
  Thus in some I read so long as leisure, in others time
  Shall teach me what I want better than words; and that with that
  Which every fair book, for all words, bears testimony.

Two loves I have, of comfort and despair,
That like two spirits do suggest me still.
My better angel is a man right fair,
Which in all that I perceive betokens death with him,
As far as he in his youth seemed;
In my heart all men were of the fair,
One of rank above the other, as in his shape.
And for my part I love those that, even now, I behold
A poor widow's husband dead; others who, to my sight alive,
Crowd together in his tomb in joy of him, all alike poor;
And all the most beloved above all I think dead.  And I love all dead that like a live
I now possess.
